Why this matters

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that interfere with functioning across school, home, and social settings (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Barkley, 2015). Simply, it is a neurological difference that affects how the brain processes motivation and attention.

What is ADHD?

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder that affects attention regulation, impulse control, and activity levels. It is commonly diagnosed in childhood but continues into adulthood for many people.

Core symptoms

The causes of ADHD are complex and involve a combination of genetic and neurological factors. Research shows strong genetic heritibility, which means ADHD often runs in families, and is linked to differences in dopamine regulation in the brain (Faraone & Biederman, 2016; Thapar et al., 2013). There may also be influences such as prenatal exposure to nicotine or alcohol and early developmental complications may also increase rise. As one of the more common methods, symptoms may be amplified by an increase short term stimulation. (Willcutt, 2012).

Important understanding

ADHD is not caused by poor parenting, laziness, or low intelligence. It is linked to differences in brain development and neurotransmitter regulation.

Real-life impact

ADHD can affect school performance, time management, emotional regulation, and relationships. However, many individuals develop strong creativity, resilience, and problem-solving skills.

The ADHD Brain

ADHD is associated with differences in dopamine regulation and activity in the prefrontal cortex, which controls planning, attention, and impulse control.

Dopamine system

Dopamine is a neurotransmitter linked to motivation and reward. In ADHD, dopamine signaling can be less consistent, making it harder to start or maintain focus on low-interest tasks.

Prefrontal cortex

This part of the brain is responsible for executive functions like planning, organizing, and controlling impulses. Differences here affect time management and task completion.

Strengths of ADHD brains

  • Creativity and idea generation
  • Hyperfocus on interesting tasks
  • Fast thinking in high-pressure situations
  • Strong problem-solving in dynamic environments

ADHD: Sociological Perspective

ADHD is not only a medical condition — it is also shaped by society. Sociology helps explain how social expectations and stigma influence lived experience.

Social norms

Schools and workplaces often expect long periods of focus, silence, and structured behaviour. These expectations define what is considered “normal” attention and behaviour.

Stigma and labelling

People with ADHD are sometimes labelled as “lazy,” “unfocused,” or “disruptive.” These labels do not reflect neurological reality and can negatively impact self-esteem.

Impact of environment

Supportive environments (flexible teaching, understanding peers, structured routines) can significantly improve outcomes for individuals with ADHD.

Key idea

ADHD is shaped by both biology and society — meaning environment plays a major role in how symptoms are experienced and understood.
