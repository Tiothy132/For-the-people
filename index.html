Why this matters

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that interfere with functioning across school, home, and social settings (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Barkley, 2015). Simply, it is a neurological difference that affects how the brain processes motivation and attention.

What is ADHD?

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder that affects attention regulation, impulse control, and activity levels. It is commonly diagnosed in childhood but continues into adulthood for many people.

Causes of ADHD

The causes of ADHD are complex and involve a combination of genetic and neurological factors. Research shows strong genetic heritability, which means ADHD often runs in families and is linked to differences in dopamine regulation in the brain (Faraone & Biederman, 2016; Thapar et al., 2013). There may also be influences such as prenatal exposure to nicotine or alcohol, and early developmental complications may also increase the risk. As one of the more common methods, symptoms may be amplified by an increase in short-term stimulation. (Willcutt, 2012).

Symptoms of ADHD

Those with ADHD may exhibit difficulty sustaining attention, disorganization, forgetfulness, impulsive decision-making, and hyperactivity or internal restlessness (American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

Treatment

There are different treatments for different people; it is more differentiated by age. For children with ADHD younger than 6 years of age, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends parent training in behaviour management as the first line of treatment, before medication is tried. For children 6 years of age and older, the recommendations include medication and behaviour therapy together, parent training in behaviour management for children up to age 12 and other types of behaviour therapy and training for adolescents. Schools can be part of the treatment as well. AAP recommendations also include adding behavioural classroom intervention and school supports. American Academy of Pediatrics. (2024, May 16).

Psychological Impacts

ADHD is associated with differences in dopamine regulation and activity in the prefrontal cortex, which controls planning, attention, and impulse control.

ADHD affects the brain systems responsible for attention, motivation, impulse control and executive function. Psychologically, ADHD is linked to differences in these areas of the pre frontal cortex, which helps with planning, organization, and such things mentioned above.

Sociological Impacts

Managing ADHD

ADHD can affect focus, organization, and daily routines, but there are many effective ways people can manage symptoms and improve daily life.

Build structured routines

Creating consistent schedules, using planners or reminder apps, and breaking tasks into smaller steps can help improve focus and time management.

Use healthy lifestyle habits

Regular exercise, enough sleep, and balanced nutrition can help regulate energy levels, attention, and mood. Physical activity is especially helpful for reducing restlessness and improving concentration.

Seek support and treatment

Therapy, coaching, school/work accommodations, and medication (when recommended by a doctor) can help people better manage ADHD symptoms and build effective coping strategies.
